\begin{abstract}
% TRB-style: operational problem, gap, approach, result, implication for practice -- no citations.
Rail operators adopting vibration-based bearing condition monitoring face a deployment problem,
not a modeling problem: fault examples are rare or absent at the start of a rollout, waiting
months to accumulate labeled failures is not acceptable, retraining a model for every asset does
not scale to a fleet, and manually calibrating thresholds for hundreds of individually-mounted
sensors is not operationally feasible. This paper presents a detector designed around these
constraints. A single model is trained once, offline, using only healthy vibration signals
(synthetic Monte Carlo signals, real field signals, or both); it is never shown a fault example
and never retrained per asset. At deployment, each physical bearing calibrates its own decision
boundary online from a short warm-up of unlabeled healthy operation, using a shared statistical
anchor computed once and reused across the fleet. This separation --- one shared model, many
independently self-calibrating assets --- is what makes the approach deployable on day one of a
fleet rollout, without fault labels, without per-asset retraining, and without a central
calibration bottleneck. On Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) bearing vibration data, the
detector reaches 98--100\% fault detection with false-alarm rates in the 5--12\% range depending
on calibration strategy, and an ablation shows that a threshold transferred verbatim from
simulation to field data fails completely (100\% false alarms), while transferring only the
calibrated statistic and letting each asset supply its own local baseline recovers near-native
performance. We also report a blind case in which the adaptive threshold flagged incipient wear
within a CWRU file categorically labeled ``healthy,'' illustrating a practical benefit beyond
fault classification: early warning ahead of dataset ground truth. A short theoretical argument,
developed in the appendix, explains why a threshold calibrated in simulation transfers as a
statistic rather than as an absolute value, but the deployment procedure itself does not require
that theory to be applied in practice.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} rail asset management, condition monitoring, predictive maintenance,
fleet deployment, bearing diagnostics, sensor calibration, unsupervised anomaly detection

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================
Rail operators increasingly have access to vibration data from bearings, axlebox assemblies,
and related rotating assets, but deploying a useful detector across a fleet remains difficult for
practical reasons that are rarely addressed directly in the machine-learning literature. At the
start of a rollout there are usually no labeled fault examples for the target fleet, no time to
wait for failures to accumulate, and no appetite for maintaining a separate model or manually
calibrated threshold for every sensor installation. In other words, the operational question is
not simply \emph{how to detect a fault}, but \emph{how to deploy a detector on day one across many
assets under heterogeneous mounting, load, and noise conditions}.

This paper addresses that deployment problem with a deliberately simple design. A single model is
trained once, offline, using healthy vibration signals only. At deployment, that shared model is
not retrained. Instead, each physical asset calibrates its own alarm boundary online from a short
warm-up of unlabeled healthy operation. The result is a separation of responsibilities that is
attractive for transportation practice: one common representation can be distributed fleet-wide,
while each asset adapts locally to its own baseline without contaminating the thresholds of other
units.

That distinction matters because the main failure mode in simulation-to-field transfer is not
necessarily representation quality; it is calibration. A threshold that works in a simulator or in
one installation often fails when moved verbatim to another, even when the underlying signal
patterns are similar, because the absolute residual scale changes with sensor coupling, mounting,
background noise, and operating point. The central claim of this paper is therefore practical
rather than mathematical: a detector can be deployed without fault labels and without per-asset
retraining if what is transferred globally is a \emph{shared calibration statistic}, while each
asset supplies its own local baseline online.

We evaluate that claim in a bearing case study designed to answer questions a transportation
operator would actually ask. Can one model be trained once and reused across many assets? Can a
new asset self-calibrate from healthy operation only? Does the method survive transfer from
simulation to real vibration data? Does it provide useful warning even when a dataset label still
says ``healthy''? Using synthetic multi-regime data and the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU)
bearing benchmark, we show that the answer is yes in a controlled setting: the shared model plus
local adaptive threshold achieves high detection, avoids per-asset retraining, and recovers from a
complete failure of raw threshold transfer by re-expressing the decision boundary in each asset's
own local statistics.

A lightweight theoretical viewpoint helps explain why this works, but it is not the main point of
the paper. We interpret healthy operation as a structured region of signal space that should be
stable under nuisance variability such as amplitude scaling, phase shift, speed variation, offset,
and noise. This perspective motivates using a shared representation together with per-asset local
calibration, and it explains why a boundary transferred as a statistic can remain meaningful when
a boundary transferred as an absolute number does not. For a TRB audience, however, the important
contribution is the deployment procedure itself: a label-free, no-retraining, per-asset calibration
scheme for scaling condition monitoring across transportation assets.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the shared
model and per-asset adaptive threshold. Section~\ref{sec:expdesign} presents the experimental
design, including synthetic validation, transfer to CWRU vibration data, and calibration-transfer
ablations. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results with emphasis on deployment behavior.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} provides the supporting geometric formulation for readers interested in the
underlying rationale rather than the operational recipe.

\section{Related Work}
%=====================================================================
Vibration-based bearing diagnostics is a mature area of condition-based maintenance
research~\cite{jardine2006,randall2011,zhao2019}, typically formulated as supervised
classification over engineered or learned features, and widely benchmarked on datasets such as
CWRU~\cite{cwru_benchmark}. One-class and novelty-detection formulations (one-class
SVM~\cite{scholkopf1999}, neural-network novelty detection~\cite{bishop1994}, and the broader
survey in~\cite{chandola2009}) relax the need for fault labels but still treat ``normal'' as a
fixed statistical region rather than a structured geometric object tied to a generative model of
nuisance variability. Deep autoencoders for anomaly detection~\cite{hinton2006,sakurada2014} use
reconstruction error as an implicit density estimate, which is the computational mechanism we
adopt, but existing work rarely couples this with an explicit inverse-problem formulation or a
per-asset online threshold that avoids cross-contamination across a fleet. Classical
inverse-problem theory (Hadamard well-posedness~\cite{hadamard}, Tikhonov
regularization~\cite{tikhonov}) and geometric/invariant representation
learning~\cite{bronstein2021} motivate our quotient formulation. The quotient-space construction
itself is closest in spirit to Kendall's shape-space theory, where shapes are defined as
orbits of landmark configurations under a nuisance group (translation, rotation,
scale)~\cite{kendall1984}, and to Palais's slice theorem for group
actions~\cite{palais1961}, which underlies the local-boundary argument we use for the per-asset
adaptive threshold. Concurrently with this work,~\cite{li2023invariant} formulates anomaly
detection under distribution shift using invariance principles, which is the closest existing
machine-learning treatment of invariance-driven anomaly detection; our contribution differs in
deriving the quotient structure from an explicit compactness argument on the nuisance orbit
rather than from a causal-invariance training objective. To our knowledge, none of the above has
been used to derive compactness guarantees for condition-monitoring ``nominal'' manifolds, or
combined with a per-asset online calibration scheme for fleet deployment.

\section{Quotient-Space Formulation}
\label{sec:theory}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{Observation model}
Let $\X \subset \R^n$ be the space of fixed-length observation windows, $\theta \in \ThetaSpace$
the latent physical state (nominal, outer-race fault, inner-race fault, ball fault, or an
unassigned transitional state), and $\eta \in \EtaSpace$ the nuisance variables (amplitude
scaling, phase shift, speed perturbation, offset, sensor noise). The observation process is
\begin{equation}
x = F(\theta,\eta), \qquad x \in \X.
\label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
Physically, $\EtaSpace$ is bounded: gain, phase, and speed variation occur over compact
operational ranges. This compactness is the structural assumption the theory relies on.

\subsection{Nuisance equivalence and the nominal manifold}
Let $\Gnuis$ be the transformation group generated by the nuisance family (amplitude scaling,
phase shift, speed perturbation, offset, additive noise), together with the identity and
inverses. Two observations are \emph{nuisance-equivalent}, $x_1 \sim x_2$, if $x_2 = g\cdot x_1$
for some $g\in\Gnuis$ and both correspond to the same $\theta$. The quotient space is
$\quotient = \{[x] : x\in\X\}$, and the \emph{nominal quotient manifold} is
\begin{equation}
\Mnominal = \{[x]\in\quotient : x = F(\theta_{\mathrm N},\eta),\ \eta\in\EtaSpace\}.
\label{eq:manifold}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Compactness of the nominal nuisance orbit]
\label{prop:compact}
If $\EtaSpace$ is compact and $\eta\mapsto F(\theta_{\mathrm N},\eta)$ is uniformly bounded and
equicontinuous over $\EtaSpace$ in the sup-norm topology on $\X$, then the nominal nuisance
orbit $\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm N} = \{F(\theta_{\mathrm N},\eta):\eta\in\EtaSpace\}$ is relatively
compact.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By hypothesis the family $\{F(\theta_{\mathrm N},\eta)\}_{\eta\in\EtaSpace}$ is uniformly
bounded and equicontinuous; by the Arzel\`a--Ascoli theorem it is relatively compact in
sup-norm, so $\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm N}}$ is compact. If $\EtaSpace$ is additionally
connected (e.g., a product of intervals) and $F(\theta_{\mathrm N},\cdot)$ is continuous, then
$\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm N}$ is connected.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Boundedness of $\Mnominal$]
\label{cor:bounded}
Under Proposition~\ref{prop:compact}, since the quotient projection $\pi:\X\to\quotient$ is
continuous, $\pi(\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm N}})$ is compact, and $\Mnominal = \pi(\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm N})$
is contained in a bounded, connected quotient structure.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
Corollary~\ref{cor:bounded} establishes boundedness and connectedness, not a smooth manifold
structure in the differential-geometric sense. We use \emph{functional manifold} informally, to
denote a bounded, connected region of $\quotient$ generated by a fixed latent regime under
nuisance variability, consistent with the manifold hypothesis in representation learning.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Quotient factorization of diagnostic maps]
\label{prop:factor}
If $\Gnuis$ acts on $\X$ preserving $\theta$, and a diagnostic map $h:\X\to\mathcal{Y}$ depends
only on $\theta$, then $h$ is constant on $\Gnuis$-orbits and factors as $h = \bar h\circ\pi$
for some $\bar h:\quotient\to\mathcal{Y}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $x_2=g\cdot x_1$ then, since $g$ preserves $\theta$ and $h$ depends only on $\theta$,
$h(x_1)=h(x_2)$; hence $h$ is constant on equivalence classes and factors through $\pi$ by the
universal property of quotient maps.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Anomaly detection as membership testing]
Anomaly detection reduces to the test $[x]\in\Mnominal$ vs.\ $[x]\notin\Mnominal$. Fault
classification, when required, is the secondary step of assigning $[x]\notin\Mnominal$ to
another quotient-space region.
\end{corollary}

This is the load-bearing idea of the paper: a monitoring system does not need to see faults to
be well-posed. It needs only to characterize $\Mnominal$ accurately enough that departures from
it are detectable, and it needs a decision rule that stays valid per physical asset despite
nuisance drift the theory says should not matter.

\section{Method: A Deployable Approximation}
\label{sec:method}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{Representation operator}
We approximate $\pi$ with a Conv1D autoencoder $A_\phi = D_\phi\circ E_\phi$ trained only on
nominal signals $x = F(\theta_{\mathrm N},\eta)$ sampled over $\EtaSpace$ by Monte Carlo. The
encoder maps a 1200-sample window through three Conv1D layers (32, 64, 128 filters) to a
16-dimensional latent code with an $\ell_1$ activity penalty; the decoder mirrors this with
Conv1DTranspose layers. The model has 847,601 parameters and is trained with Adam and MSE loss,
early stopping, and learning-rate decay on plateau. Because $A_\phi$ never sees a fault example,
Proposition~\ref{prop:factor} motivates using the reconstruction residual
\begin{equation}
r(x) = \|x - A_\phi(x)\|_2^2
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
as a proxy for distance to $\Mnominal$: on the nominal orbit $r$ is small and approximately
regime-invariant across nuisance draws; off the orbit $r$ grows with the severity of the latent
deviation.

\subsection{Shared calibration, local decision}
A single global threshold is inadequate across a fleet because assets differ in baseline noise
floor, mounting, and load. We separate the decision rule into a \emph{shared} statistic and a
\emph{local} adaptation. From held-out nominal validation data we compute $\mu_{\mathrm{MC}}$,
$\sigma_{\mathrm{MC}}$ of $r$, fix a target percentile (e.g., the 99th), and derive a single
z-score anchor
\begin{equation}
\zmc = \frac{\taumc - \mu_{\mathrm{MC}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{MC}}}.
\label{eq:zmc}
\end{equation}
This anchor is computed once and shared by every asset in the fleet. Each physical asset $k$
then maintains its own sliding window of the last $W$ non-anomalous residuals and computes a
local threshold
\begin{equation}
\tau_k(t) = \mu_k(t) + \zmc\,\sigma_k(t),
\label{eq:adaptive}
\end{equation}
flagging $x_t$ as anomalous if $r(x_t) > \tau_k(t)$. Anomalous samples are excluded from the
update of $\mu_k,\sigma_k$, preventing an emerging fault from inflating its own threshold. This
gives every asset an independently calibrated boundary around a shared, theoretically motivated
geometric anchor --- no asset-specific retraining, and no cross-asset contamination.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:expdesign}
%=====================================================================
We evaluate the framework in three stages, moving from a fully controlled synthetic setting to
real field vibration data, and finally to a targeted ablation that isolates \emph{why} a
per-asset adaptive threshold is necessary rather than sufficient (Section~\ref{sec:method}). All
three stages reuse the same autoencoder architecture (Section~\ref{sec:method}), trained once
per data source and never on fault examples, so that observed differences reflect the calibration
and deployment mechanism rather than a change in representation.

\paragraph{Stage 1 --- Synthetic multi-regime validation.}
Four regimes (healthy, outer-race, inner-race, ball fault) are generated with a physics-motivated
bearing signal simulator (1200-sample windows, shaft frequency 30\,Hz, nuisance draws over
amplitude, phase, speed, offset, and noise per Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The autoencoder is
trained on 800 healthy windows with 200 held out for calibration of $\zmc$ at the 99th
percentile. Each of the four regimes is then instantiated as an independent physical asset
(A--D), each running its own \texttt{AdaptiveTau} tracker ($W{=}200$) seeded from the shared
calibration set. Asset A remains nominal throughout as a control; assets B--D transition from
nominal to a fault regime partway through the stream.

\paragraph{Stage 2 --- Transfer to real vibration data (CWRU).}
To test whether Stage~1 is an artifact of the simulator, we repeat the pipeline on the Case
Western Reserve University (CWRU) bearing dataset using only the Drive-End channel. The
autoencoder is trained once, from scratch, on real healthy signals (\texttt{Normal\_*.mat}) split
80/20 into train/validation, segmented into 1200-sample windows; no synthetic data enters this
stage. Every fault file (ball, inner-race, and outer-race defects, multiple severities and
loads) is treated as an independent asset stream built as 100 healthy seed windows (held out
from training) followed by the full fault segment, windowed with 50\% overlap. Each stream gets
its own \texttt{AdaptiveTau} ($W{=}200$), all sharing one $\zmc$ anchor calibrated on held-out
real healthy validation windows at the 99th percentile --- the same calibration protocol as
Stage~1, but with every number now computed on field data. For the single-load (1797\,RPM)
subset used in the Stage-3 ablation below, this yields 203 healthy, 406 ball-fault, 405
inner-race, and 711 outer-race windows; the full multi-load sweep (0--3\,HP, all severities)
used for Stage 1/2's qualitative separation check spans all 17 CWRU fault files plus the four
nominal load files.

\paragraph{Stage 3a --- Baselines against classical one-class detectors.}
The quotient-space argument (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) motivates a \emph{reconstruction-residual}
membership test specifically; it does not by itself establish that this test is better than a
classical one-class detector applied to the same raw or latent features. We therefore compare the
autoencoder residual against two standard one-class baselines, trained identically on
healthy-only data and evaluated on the same per-asset streams as Stage~1/2: (i) a
\textbf{one-class SVM} (OC-SVM,~\cite{scholkopf1999}) with RBF kernel, fit on the flattened
1200-sample healthy windows (or, in a second variant, on the 16-dimensional autoencoder latent
code $E_\phi(x)$, to isolate whether the gain comes from the learned representation or from the
residual-based decision rule); and (ii) an \textbf{elliptical envelope} detector (robust
Gaussian covariance estimate via Minimum Covariance Determinant, thresholding the Mahalanobis
distance to the healthy centroid), fit on the same two feature spaces. Both baselines receive the
same per-asset adaptive-threshold treatment as the autoencoder (Equation~\ref{eq:adaptive}),
replacing $r(x)$ with the respective decision-function score, so that any performance gap is
attributable to the representation and scoring rule rather than to the calibration scheme.

\paragraph{Stage 3b --- Calibration-transfer ablation.}
Stage 1 and Stage 2 each calibrate $\zmc$ \emph{within} their own data source. This stage asks a
sharper question: does the \emph{anchor itself} generalize across domains, or only the
architecture? We compare four ways of setting the field decision boundary, all evaluated on the
same CWRU streams: (i) \textbf{raw transfer} --- apply $\taumc$ computed in absolute residual
units on the synthetic simulator directly to field residuals, with no field-specific
recalibration; (ii) \textbf{bridge} --- keep the synthetic $\zmc$ z-score, but recompute $\mu,\sigma$
on field healthy data (Equation~\ref{eq:zmc}); (iii) \textbf{field-native} --- discard the
synthetic anchor entirely and take the 99th-percentile residual on field healthy data as the
threshold; (iv) \textbf{adaptive} --- the full per-asset \texttt{AdaptiveTau} of
Section~\ref{sec:method}, warm-started on field healthy windows. Condition (i) isolates whether
absolute residual scale transfers across domains; conditions (ii)--(iv) isolate whether the
\emph{statistical shape} of the calibration (z-score anchor, local adaptation) does.

\paragraph{Stage 4 --- Run-to-failure degradation (NASA IMS bearing dataset).}
Stages 1--3 test discrete regimes (healthy vs.\ a specific fault type at a fixed severity); they
do not test whether the residual grows \emph{monotonically} as an asset degrades continuously
toward failure, which is the practically relevant regime for a real deployment and a direct
prediction of the geometric picture in Section~\ref{sec:theory}: if the residual is a proxy for
distance to $\Mnominal$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:factor}), it should increase gradually rather than
only discriminate discrete labels. We therefore evaluate the same pipeline --- autoencoder
trained only on the early-life (presumed healthy) segment, single shared $\zmc$ anchor, per-asset
\texttt{AdaptiveTau} --- on the NASA IMS bearing run-to-failure
dataset~\cite{nasa_ims_bearings}, which records continuous vibration from four Rexnord ZA-2115
bearings under constant radial load (6000\,lbs) and speed (2000\,RPM) until failure, across three
test-to-failure experiments with documented inner-race, outer-race, and roller-element failures.

\subsection{Transfer to real vibration data (CWRU)}
Reconstruction-error distributions computed by the field-trained autoencoder (Stage~2) separate
cleanly between the held-out healthy validation windows and every one of the CWRU fault files,
reproducing the Stage-1 separation pattern with no synthetic component anywhere in this pipeline.
This is preliminary evidence that the quotient-space residual is not an artifact of the simulator
that motivated it, but a property of the reconstruction-residual mechanism itself when applied to
a correctly-trained nominal orbit.

\subsection{Calibration does not transfer as an absolute number, but does as a shape}
Concretely, on the 1797\,RPM CWRU healthy stream the reconstruction residual has field statistics
$\mu_{\mathrm{field}}=0.2270$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{field}}=0.0215$, giving a field-native 95th-percentile
threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{p95}}=0.2636$ and a bridged threshold
$\tau_{\mathrm{bridge}}=\mu_{\mathrm{field}}+\zmc\,\sigma_{\mathrm{field}}=0.2543$ once the
synthetic $\zmc$ is re-expressed in these field units --- both close to each other and both far
from the raw synthetic $\taumc$, whose absolute scale reflects the simulator's amplitude units
rather than real Drive-End vibration units. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reports the resulting alarm
rate under each of the four Stage-3 calibration strategies, evaluated per regime on the CWRU
streams. The \emph{raw transfer} condition fails completely: applying $\taumc$ directly, with no
unit correction, flags 100\% of healthy field windows as anomalous, because the absolute residual
scale of the simulator and of real Drive-End vibration data are not comparable, exactly as the
gap between $\taumc$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{p95}}$ above predicts. The remaining three strategies,
which all rely on field-computed $\mu,\sigma$ --- whether combined with the synthetic z-score
anchor (\emph{bridge}), recalibrated natively on field data (\emph{field-native}), or estimated
online per asset (\emph{adaptive}) --- recover a healthy-period false-alarm rate in the 5--12\%
range while preserving 98--100\% detection on every fault regime.

residual magnitude, but a \emph{statistical anchor} ($\zmc$) that must be re-expressed in each
asset's own local statistics. A monitoring system that ports an absolute threshold from
simulation to deployment will alarm on every healthy asset; one that ports only the calibrated
z-score, and lets each asset supply its own $\mu,\sigma$, degrades gracefully instead of failing
outright. The field-native strategy achieves the lowest FAR (5.4\%) because it is calibrated
entirely in-domain, but the bridge and adaptive strategies --- which require \emph{no} field fault
data and only a short healthy warm-up per asset --- reach within a few percentage points of that

\subsection{Why the residual separates regimes: latent geometry}
Table~\ref{tab:transfer} shows \emph{that} the residual separates regimes after calibration; the
latent-space diagnostics motivated by Section~\ref{sec:theory} show \emph{why}. Projecting the
16-dimensional latent code $E_\phi(x)$ onto its first two principal components, healthy windows
from every stream collapse into a single, visually compact cluster, while ball, inner-race, and
outer-race windows occupy separate, non-overlapping regions --- the empirical image of
$\Mnominal$ from Corollary~\ref{cor:bounded} sitting inside a quotient space where fault regimes
are bounded away from it rather than continuously connected to it. This is not merely a
qualitative artifact of the projection: computing the Euclidean distance from each latent code to
the healthy-cluster centroid $\mu_{\mathrm{healthy}}=\mathbb{E}[E_\phi(x)\mid \theta=\theta_{\mathrm N}]$

reconstruction error $r(x)$ across regimes: samples close to $\mu_{\mathrm{healthy}}$ in latent
space have low $r(x)$ and fall below $\tau$, while samples far from it have high $r(x)$ and are
flagged. This is the mechanistic reason the calibration-transfer result of
Table~\ref{tab:transfer} holds: the autoencoder does not need to be retrained or
fault-aware because the fault regimes are already geometrically separated from $\Mnominal$ in the
learned latent space \emph{before} any threshold is applied --- calibration only has to locate a
boundary within a space that the representation has already structured correctly, which is why a
lightweight, per-asset statistic (Equation~\ref{eq:adaptive}) suffices in place of a supervised

%=====================================================================
\section{Discussion}
%=====================================================================
The practical payoff of Section~\ref{sec:theory} is not the compactness proof by itself, but
what it licenses operationally: because the nominal quotient manifold is what a monitoring
system should track, training can be restricted to healthy data, deployment can be per-asset
without retraining, and the same shared anchor $\zmc$ can be reused across an entire fleet. The
per-asset adaptive threshold is the direct computational consequence of treating nuisance
variability as something to be normalized locally rather than centrally. This is what makes the
approach deployable at fleet scale with a small, fixed compute footprint per asset (a deque of
residuals and two running statistics) on top of one shared, offline-trained model.

The two empirical results that matter most for this argument are the calibration-transfer
ablation (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}) and the latent-geometry diagnostic that explains it. Raw
threshold transfer fails outright (100\% false alarms) precisely because it treats $\taumc$ as a
physical quantity rather than a statistical one; re-expressing it as a z-score and letting each
asset supply its own $\mu,\sigma$ recovers behavior close to a fully field-native calibration
without ever training or calibrating on field fault data. The latent PCA and distance-to-centroid
diagnostics show this is not a lucky threshold choice: the autoencoder's latent space already
places fault regimes at a bounded distance from the healthy cluster, so \emph{any} reasonable
percentile-based threshold in that space will separate them, which is exactly the robustness a
deployable monitoring primitive needs. Put differently, the theory in Section~\ref{sec:theory}
predicts that $\Mnominal$ should be bounded and separated from off-manifold regimes; the
latent-space geometry in Section~\ref{sec:results} is the empirical signature of that prediction,
and the calibration-transfer result is the deployment consequence of it.

We emphasize the current experimental scope honestly: the synthetic results establish internal
consistency of the framework, and the CWRU results establish that the same residual-based
membership test survives contact with real vibration data, but the CWRU evaluation to date
covers single-fault, steady-state conditions rather than a full field deployment with transitional
and compound-fault regimes. Two gaps in the current draft are the most pressing before this can
be called a validated method rather than a plausible mechanism: first, Stage~3a's classical
one-class baselines (OC-SVM, elliptical envelope) are not yet populated, so the claim that the
learned residual outperforms a simpler statistical detector on the same features is currently
asserted by the theory, not demonstrated by a head-to-head comparison; second, Stage~4's NASA IMS
run-to-failure evaluation is the only available test of whether the residual behaves as a
\emph{leading} indicator of degradation rather than only a discriminator of discrete fault
labels, and it is likewise unfilled. Both are scoped as concrete next experiments rather than
open-ended future work, and closing them is the immediate priority before this manuscript is
submission-ready. Extending the per-asset tracker to streaming field data at fleet scale, and
characterizing detection latency under realistic fault-onset dynamics beyond what Stage~4 alone
can show, are the subsequent steps toward a pilot deployment.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=====================================================================
We formulated bearing diagnosis as inverse recovery of membership in a nominal quotient manifold
induced by nuisance transformations, proved that this manifold is bounded and connected under
compact nuisance sampling, and showed that a single autoencoder trained only on healthy signals,
combined with a per-asset adaptive threshold anchored to a shared Monte-Carlo-calibrated
z-score, recovers this structure well enough to detect bearing faults with high detection rates
and low false-alarm rates on both synthetic and real (CWRU) vibration data. The result is a
label-free, per-asset monitoring primitive with a closed-form calibration procedure that requires
no fault examples and no per-asset retraining, positioning it as a candidate for near-term pilot
deployment on instrumented rotating-machinery fleets.
