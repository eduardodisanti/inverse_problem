\begin{abstract}

Transportation operators increasingly face the challenge of deploying condition-monitoring systems across large fleets of heterogeneous assets without fault labels, asset-specific retraining, or lengthy calibration campaigns. We propose a day-one deployment method that explicitly separates what is shared across a fleet from what must remain local to each operational asset. A single nominal representation is trained once, offline, using only healthy observations, and is subsequently deployed unchanged across all assets. At deployment, each asset independently estimates its own operational radius from a short stream of unlabeled nominal observations while preserving the shared representation. We hypothesize that, under stable operation, this radius converges rapidly to an asset-specific
value; after convergence, persistent departures from the converged radius are interpreted as evidence of degradation rather than absorbed through continued adaptation. The proposed deployment strategy is representation-agnostic and does not rely on fault labels or per-asset retraining. It is validated on public rolling-element bearing datasets, where it preserves 98--100\% fault detection while demonstrating that transferring an absolute threshold from simulation fails catastrophically, whereas transferring only a standardized calibration anchor and re-expressing it in each asset's local statistics recovers near-native performance. The resulting deployment primitive consists of a shared nominal representation, per-asset operational-radius convergence, and lightweight edge inference. A compact theoretical rationale is provided in the appendix as an explanatory justification rather than as a prerequisite for deployment.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%=====================================================================

Modern transportation systems increasingly rely on condition-monitoring technologies to supervise heterogeneous operational assets, including rolling-element bearings, axleboxes, traction motors, gearboxes, pumps, actuators, and other components whose condition can be inferred from repeated observations. Despite the rapid progress of machine learning for anomaly detection, deploying a monitoring system across an operational fleet remains fundamentally a deployment problem rather than a representation-learning problem.

At fleet rollout, four constraints typically occur simultaneously. First, the target fleet contains few or no labeled fault examples. Second, waiting months or years for representative failures is operationally unacceptable. Third, installation-specific factors such as sensor coupling, mounting, operating conditions, and environmental noise modify the numerical scale of anomaly scores from one asset to another. Fourth, maintaining a separate model or manually calibrated threshold for every operational unit does not scale operationally.

The practical question is therefore not simply

\begin{quote}
\emph{How can a fault be detected?}
\end{quote}

but rather

\begin{quote}
\emph{How can a shared condition-monitoring model be deployed on day one across many heterogeneous assets without fault labels, per-asset retraining, or manual threshold tuning?}
\end{quote}

We address this deployment problem by separating the diagnostic representation from the local decision boundary. The representation is learned once, offline, and shared across the fleet. Calibration remains local: each operational asset estimates its own operational radius from a short warm-up of unlabeled nominal observations while preserving the common representation. 
Under stable operation, the proposed calibration process is expected to drive each asset toward
its own operational radius,
\[
\tau_k(t)\rightarrow\tau_k^\star,
\]
without modifying the shared model. This convergence hypothesis is evaluated experimentally in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

Once convergence has been reached, the radius is frozen or tightly bounded so that progressive degradation cannot redefine itself as nominal.

This distinction is motivated by a practical observation. Simulation-to-field transfer frequently fails because calibration does not transfer, even when the learned representation remains valid. Absolute anomaly thresholds depend on installation-specific score scales, whereas standardized statistical anchors can be transferred and subsequently re-expressed using local residual statistics. The deployment procedure proposed here therefore transfers only the calibration rule, not the absolute decision boundary.

The contribution of this paper is consequently a deployment methodology rather than a new neural-network architecture. Although the experimental validation uses a lightweight Conv1D autoencoder as the shared nominal representation, the deployment mechanism itself is independent of the underlying representation model. The principal contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item a day-one deployment architecture based on one shared nominal representation and independently calibrated operational assets;
\item a standardized simulator- or validation-derived calibration anchor that initializes local decision boundaries without transferring absolute score scales;
\item an operational-radius convergence criterion that separates cold-start calibration from subsequent degradation monitoring;
\item a guarded adaptation mechanism that prevents anomalous observations from inflating their own acceptance boundary; and
\item an experimental protocol that evaluates cold-start behaviour, convergence, simulation-to-field transfer, and post-convergence anomaly detection.
\end{enumerate}

The methodology is intentionally asset-agnostic. It requires only a shared nominal representation together with repeated observations from independently monitored operational units. In this paper, we validate the approach using public rolling-element bearing datasets (CWRU and NASA IMS), which provide controlled nominal, fault, and run-to-failure conditions. Bearings should therefore be interpreted as the first experimental validation domain rather than as a limitation of the proposed deployment methodology.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews condition monitoring, one-class anomaly detection, adaptive calibration, and fleet deployment. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the proposed deployment methodology. Section~\ref{sec:expdesign} describes the experimental protocol. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the deployment-oriented results, while Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications, limitations, and future extensions. A compact theoretical justification is provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:theory}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%=====================================================================

Condition-based maintenance (CBM) has become a central component of modern transportation asset
management, with condition monitoring providing the operational evidence required to supervise
large fleets of heterogeneous assets \cite{jardine2006,zhao2019}. Although the sensing modality
depends on the application---including vibration, acoustic emission, electrical current,
temperature, strain, or other physical measurements---the computational objective is
fundamentally the same: to characterize nominal operation and detect persistent departures that
may indicate degradation.

Most machine-learning approaches formulate this problem as supervised fault classification,
assuming representative fault examples are available during training. While effective in
benchmark datasets, supervised methods require fault labels and often assume that deployment
conditions closely match those observed during model development.

One-class and novelty-detection methods, including one-class SVM
\cite{scholkopf1999}, neural-network novelty detection \cite{bishop1994}, and autoencoder-based
anomaly detection \cite{hinton2006,sakurada2014}, reduce the dependence on fault labels by
learning only nominal behaviour. Existing work primarily studies how to learn anomaly detectors,
whereas comparatively little attention has been paid to the deployment problem itself: how a
single nominal model should be initialized, calibrated, and maintained across a heterogeneous
fleet of independently operating assets.

Adaptive thresholds are frequently employed in streaming anomaly detection to compensate for
changes in operating conditions. However, unrestricted adaptation introduces an important
operational risk: persistent degradation may progressively redefine itself as nominal by
continuously shifting the decision boundary. This observation motivates separating calibration
from monitoring. In the proposed methodology, adaptation is restricted to a finite cold-start
phase whose objective is to estimate an operational radius for each asset. Once convergence has
been achieved, the shared representation remains fixed and subsequent departures become
diagnostic evidence rather than input for further adaptation.

The present work is also related to invariant representation learning
\cite{bronstein2021} and anomaly detection under distribution shift
\cite{li2023invariant}, which seek representations that are insensitive to operationally
irrelevant variability. Our contribution lies at a different layer of the condition-monitoring pipeline.
 Rather than proposing a new
representation-learning architecture, we address the operational deployment problem itself:
how a shared nominal representation can be deployed across heterogeneous assets through
standardized initialization, independent operational-radius convergence, and local calibration,
without fault labels or per-asset retraining.

The methodology is intentionally independent of both sensing modality and asset type. Public
rolling-element bearing datasets (CWRU and NASA IMS) are used in this paper solely as controlled
experimental benchmarks because they provide nominal operation, multiple fault modes, and
run-to-failure data under well-characterized conditions.

Accordingly, this paper should be interpreted not as proposing another anomaly-detection architecture, but as introducing a deployment methodology in which a shared nominal representation is combined with independent operational-radius convergence for each monitored asset.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{Deployment architecture: shared representation, local radius}

Let $x_{k,t}\in\X$ denote an observation window acquired from operational unit $k$ at time
$t$. In the bearing validation presented in this paper, $x_{k,t}$ is instantiated as a
fixed-length vibration window. A shared
offline-trained representation model produces an anomaly score
\begin{equation}
r_{k,t}=r(x_{k,t}).
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

The representation model is common to the fleet. The decision boundary is not. Each asset
maintains a local operational radius $\tau_k(t)$ and raises an alarm when
\begin{equation}
r_{k,t}>\tau_k(t).
\label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}

The method therefore enforces three deployment rules:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item the representation is shared;
    \item the calibration is local;
    \item the model is never retrained per asset.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}[Operational radius]
For a fixed nominal representation and an independently monitored operational unit $k$, the
operational radius $\tau_k^\star$ is the converged, asset-specific acceptance boundary estimated
from nominal deployment observations. It calibrates the anomaly decision locally without
modifying or retraining the shared representation.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Representation instantiation}

The deployment method is independent of the representation backbone. In this paper, we
instantiate it with a Conv1D autoencoder $A_\phi=D_\phi\circ E_\phi$ trained exclusively on
nominal signals. The encoder maps a 1200-sample window through three Conv1D layers
(32, 64, and 128 filters) to a 16-dimensional latent code with an $\ell_1$ activity penalty; the
decoder mirrors this structure with Conv1DTranspose layers. The model has 847,601 parameters
and is trained with Adam, mean-squared reconstruction loss, early stopping, and learning-rate
decay.

The anomaly score is the reconstruction residual
\begin{equation}
r(x)=\|x-A_\phi(x)\|_2^2.
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}

The autoencoder is an implementation choice, not the scientific contribution. Any nominal
representation producing a stable scalar distance or anomaly score could replace
Equation~\ref{eq:residual}.

\subsection{Standardized anchor for day-one initialization}

From held-out nominal validation data or a nominal simulator, we estimate the residual mean
$\mu_0$ and standard deviation $\sigma_0$, choose a target nominal percentile, and compute the
standardized anchor
\begin{equation}
z_0=\frac{\tau_0-\mu_0}{\sigma_0}.
\label{eq:zanchor}
\end{equation}

The absolute value $\tau_0$ is not transferred to deployment. Only the dimensionless anchor
$z_0$ is shared across the fleet.

For a newly deployed asset $k$, the local threshold is
\begin{equation}
\tau_k(t)=\mu_k(t)+z_0\,\sigma_k(t),
\label{eq:adaptive}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_k(t)$ and $\sigma_k(t)$ are estimated from the asset's accepted nominal residuals.

This construction separates the shape of the fleet-wide decision rule from the local residual
scale induced by mounting, sensor gain, load, and environmental noise.

\subsection{Operational-radius convergence}

During cold start, each asset collects a short sequence of accepted nominal residuals and updates
Equation~\ref{eq:adaptive}. The asset is considered calibrated when the radius stabilizes.

For a look-back interval $L$, define the convergence statistic
\begin{equation}
C_k(t)=
\frac{\left|\tau_k(t)-\tau_k(t-L)\right|}
{\max\left(\tau_k(t-L),\epsilon\right)},
\label{eq:convergence}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon>0$ prevents division by zero.

Calibration is declared when
\begin{equation}
C_k(t)<\varepsilon_C
\end{equation}
for $m$ consecutive updates. The converged operational radius is then
\begin{equation}
\tau_k^\star=\tau_k(t_{\mathrm{conv}}).
\label{eq:taustar}
\end{equation}

The key hypothesis evaluated in this paper is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Under stable nominal operation, every asset converges rapidly to its own operational
radius around a fixed shared representation.}
\end{quote}

After convergence, $\tau_k^\star$ is frozen or allowed only a tightly bounded robust correction.
This prevents a slowly increasing anomaly score from being absorbed as a new nominal state.

\subsection{Guarded updates}

Only confidently nominal samples are admitted to the local baseline. Let $\delta>0$ define a
guard band. A residual updates the local statistics only when
\begin{equation}
r_{k,t}<\tau_k(t)-\delta.
\label{eq:safe-update}
\end{equation}

Samples inside the guard band are observed but do not update the baseline, and samples above
$\tau_k(t)$ are flagged as anomalous. The three regions are therefore:

\begin{align}
r_{k,t}<\tau_k(t)-\delta
&\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{safe update},\\
\tau_k(t)-\delta\le r_{k,t}\le\tau_k(t)
&\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{observe, do not update},\\
r_{k,t}>\tau_k(t)
&\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{anomaly}.
\end{align}

This rule is deliberately lightweight: each asset stores only a residual buffer or running robust
statistics, its current radius, and its convergence state.

\subsection{Deployment states}

Each asset transitions through three operational states:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Cold start:} the standardized anchor is re-expressed in local statistics;
    \item \textbf{Converged nominal:} $\tau_k^\star$ has stabilized and monitoring is active;
    \item \textbf{Departure:} persistent scores exceed the converged radius.
\end{enumerate}

A practical alarm may require $q$ exceedances within the last $p$ observations to avoid reacting
to isolated impulses.

\subsection{Scope of the Method}

The deployment mechanism proposed in this paper is intentionally asset-agnostic.
Throughout the paper, the index $k$ denotes an independently monitored operational
unit. Depending on the application, $k$ may represent a rolling-element bearing,
a traction motor, a gearbox, a pump, an actuator, a vehicle, or a spatially
indexed infrastructure segment.

The methodology requires only:
(i) a shared nominal representation,
(ii) repeated observations from each operational unit, and
(iii) local estimation of an operational radius.

The present manuscript validates the approach on public bearing datasets because
they provide controlled nominal, fault, and run-to-failure conditions. Bearings
should therefore be interpreted as the first validation domain rather than as a
limitation of the proposed deployment method.


\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:expdesign}
%=====================================================================

The experiments are designed around the deployment claim, not around the autoencoder
architecture. Every stage therefore measures some subset of the following quantities:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item false-alarm rate during cold start;
    \item number of accepted samples to operational-radius convergence;
    \item variability of $\tau_k^\star$ across assets and regimes;
    \item post-convergence detection rate;
    \item sensitivity to buffer size and convergence parameters;
    \item performance of absolute-threshold transfer versus standardized-anchor transfer;
    \item cost of per-asset retraining avoided by the shared-model design.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Stage 1 --- Controlled multi-asset simulation.}
Four bearing streams are generated with a physics-motivated nominal simulator using
1200-sample windows, a 30\,Hz shaft frequency, and bounded variation in amplitude, phase, speed,
offset, and noise. The shared model is trained on 800 nominal windows, with 200 held out to
derive $z_0$. Each simulated asset begins in nominal operation and independently calibrates its
radius. Asset A remains nominal; Assets B--D transition after convergence to outer-race,
inner-race, and ball-fault regimes.

This stage measures:
(i) samples to convergence;
(ii) false alarms before and after convergence;
(iii) the separation between $r_{k,t}$ and $\tau_k^\star$ after fault onset; and
(iv) whether each nominal asset reaches a stable but asset-specific radius.

\paragraph{Stage 2 --- Direct simulation-to-field deployment on CWRU.}
The nominal representation trained in Stage~1 exclusively on synthetic healthy signals is
transferred unchanged to the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) Drive-End data. No CWRU
signal---healthy or faulty---is used to retrain, fine-tune, or otherwise modify the representation
model. The purpose of this stage is to separate representation transfer from decision-boundary
transfer.

Each CWRU operating condition is treated as an independent deployment environment. A short
stream of unlabeled healthy observations is used only to estimate the local residual statistics
$\mu_k(t)$ and $\sigma_k(t)$ and, consequently, the asset-specific operational radius
$\tau_k(t)$. Fault observations are then evaluated using the same simulator-trained model and
the locally calibrated decision boundary.

This stage tests two distinct hypotheses:
(i) whether the nominal representation learned from simulation transfers directly to real field
signals without retraining; and
(ii) whether the standardized calibration anchor transfers even though the absolute simulator
threshold does not.

The main outcome is not merely fault classification. It is whether each stream reaches a stable
$\tau_k^\star$ during warm-up and then detects the subsequent departure without model retraining.

\paragraph{Stage 3a --- Baselines.}
Throughout this ablation, the simulator-trained representation model is held fixed. Only the
decision-boundary calibration strategy is changed.
We compare the autoencoder residual against:
(i) one-class SVM on raw windows;
(ii) one-class SVM on latent codes;
(iii) elliptical envelope on raw features; and
(iv) elliptical envelope on latent codes.
Every detector uses the same standardized-anchor and local-radius procedure so that differences
are attributable to the representation and score rather than to unequal calibration.

\paragraph{Stage 3b --- Calibration-transfer ablation.}
We compare four deployment strategies:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Absolute transfer:} deploy the simulator threshold $\tau_0$ unchanged;
    \item \textbf{Standardized bridge:} transfer $z_0$ and estimate local $\mu_k,\sigma_k$;
    \item \textbf{Field-native offline:} estimate a percentile threshold from held-out healthy
    field data;
    \item \textbf{Online local convergence:} initialize with $z_0$ and estimate $\tau_k^\star$
    during a short unlabeled warm-up.
\end{enumerate}

This ablation directly tests whether the transferable quantity is an absolute threshold or a
standardized calibration rule.

This ablation separates representation transfer from calibration transfer. The representation
is transferred unchanged from simulation to field deployment; the experiment tests whether the
transferable calibration quantity is the absolute threshold or its standardized position within
the nominal residual distribution.

\paragraph{Stage 4 --- Run-to-failure degradation on NASA IMS.}
An independently trained nominal representation is constructed from an early-life segment
presumed to correspond to healthy operation for the IMS bearing family. The proposed deployment
methodology is then applied unchanged: each bearing independently calibrates its operational
radius during the initial nominal period and subsequently enters post-convergence monitoring.
We report:
(i) whether $\tau_k(t)$ converges during early life;
(ii) whether residuals depart from the frozen radius before documented failure;
(iii) alarm lead time; and
(iv) false alarms during the accepted nominal period.

The critical distinction is between an adapting threshold and a converged operational radius.
After $t_{\mathrm{conv}}$, the boundary is not allowed to track the degradation trajectory.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%=====================================================================

The principal result is not that a Conv1D autoencoder can separate CWRU fault classes. That
finding is already well established in the literature. The contribution is a deployment rule for
heterogeneous transportation fleets: the nominal representation can be shared, while every asset
independently identifies a local operational radius without model retraining.

The standardized bridge experiment shows why this separation is necessary. The numerical scale
of reconstruction residuals is installation-dependent. A simulator threshold therefore fails when
treated as a physical quantity. Its standardized location within a nominal distribution is more
portable, provided the deployed asset estimates its own local scale.

Operational-radius convergence also clarifies the role of adaptation. The purpose of adaptation
is to identify the asset during cold start, not to follow its aging indefinitely. Once
$\tau_k^\star$ is established, continued movement of the anomaly-score distribution is
diagnostic information. Free adaptation after convergence would instead risk absorbing a
progressive fault into the nominal baseline.

For transportation practice, this architecture has several advantages. It supports day-one
rollout without fault labels, removes per-asset model maintenance, isolates calibration between
physical units, permits edge execution with minimal local state, and reduces continuous transfer of high-frequency raw sensor observations.
The same deployment principle can be applied to bearings,
traction motors, gearboxes, pumps, actuators, and spatially indexed infrastructure segments,
provided the shared representation is trained for the relevant signal family.

The present evaluation remains limited. CWRU is a controlled benchmark rather than an
in-service railway fleet, and fault streams are constructed rather than observed during natural
field evolution. NASA IMS provides a stronger run-to-failure test but still represents laboratory
rotating machinery. A later transportation-specific validation should apply the same deployment
procedure to instrumented axlebox, tram, or train data with repeated observations and maintenance
records. That extension is important for external validity but is not required to test the central
calibration mechanism presented here.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=====================================================================

We presented a day-one deployment methodology for nominal condition monitoring across
heterogeneous asset fleets, validated in this study on public rolling-element bearing data. A
single representation is trained once on simulated nominal observations and transferred unchanged
to field deployment. Each operational unit then uses a short unlabeled warm-up to identify its
own operational radius without modifying or retraining the shared model. The experiments show
that the learned representation reuse from simulation to field data, whereas the absolute
decision threshold does not. A standardized calibration anchor, re-expressed in each asset's
local residual statistics, provides the bridge between the shared representation and local
deployment. The resulting primitive is label-free, independently calibrated, compatible with
edge inference, and designed around fleet deployment rather than benchmark-specific
classification.

\appendix
\section{Geometric Rationale}
\label{sec:theory}
%=====================================================================

This appendix provides a compact explanation for why a fixed nominal representation and local
operational radii are compatible. It is not required for implementation.

\subsection{Observation model}

Let $\X\subset\R^n$ be the space of fixed-length observation windows, $\theta\in\ThetaSpace$
the latent physical state, and $\eta\in\EtaSpace$ the nuisance variables. The observation process
is
\begin{equation}
x=F(\theta,\eta).
\end{equation}

Let $\Gnuis$ denote a physically motivated family of nuisance transformations that preserve the
underlying diagnostic state. Two observations are equivalent when they differ only through such
a transformation.

\subsection{Compact nominal structure}

\begin{proposition}[Relative compactness of the nominal orbit]
If $\EtaSpace$ is compact and $\eta\mapsto F(\theta_{\mathrm N},\eta)$ is uniformly bounded and
equicontinuous in the sup norm, then the nominal orbit
\[
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm N}=
\{F(\theta_{\mathrm N},\eta):\eta\in\EtaSpace\}
\]
is relatively compact.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The result follows directly from the Arzel\`a--Ascoli theorem. Therefore
$\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm N}}$ is compact.
\end{proof}

A representation that collapses nuisance-equivalent observations can therefore map nominal
operation into a bounded structure. The shared representation approximates this structure,
whereas the local radius accounts for asset-specific score scale and dispersion. Estimating
$\tau_k^\star$ does not deform the nominal representation; it only identifies the local
acceptance boundary around it.

\begin{proposition}[Factorization of diagnostic maps]
If a diagnostic map is invariant under $\Gnuis$, then it is constant on nuisance orbits and
factors through the quotient projection.
\end{proposition}

This proposition motivates treating anomaly detection as membership testing in a nominal
representation. It does not prescribe the autoencoder, the residual, or the calibration rule used
in the main paper.
